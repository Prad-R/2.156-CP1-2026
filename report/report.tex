\section{Summary}

Our final submission scores 3.69 with the course scorer (hypervolumes 5.87,
8.52 and 24.60 for the three kangaroos). The advanced starter notebook, run
unchanged, gave us 0.045, so nearly all of that had to come from somewhere
else. What ended up working was not a cleverer optimizer but a lot of random
guessing followed by ordinary gradient descent, and then a step that lets
mechanisms grow one joint at a time. Section~\ref{sec:approach} goes through
these in the order we tried them, Section~\ref{sec:error} covers the things we
tried that did not work, and Section~\ref{sec:results} has the final fronts and
mechanisms. The short version of what we could not do: we never managed to
trace the tail of Kangaroo~3.

\section{The problem as we read it}

For each target curve we can submit up to 1000 mechanisms with at most 20
joints. Each one is scored on distance (how far the traced curve is from the
target after the best rotation and starting point) and material (total link
length), both minimized. A mechanism only counts if it is strictly under both
limits in Table~\ref{tab:problems}, and the score per target is the hypervolume
with respect to those limits divided by a normalizer. The overall score is the
mean of the three.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{llccc}
\toprule
Problem & Target & Distance limit & Material limit & Normalizer \\
\midrule
1 & Kangaroo 1 (round body) & 0.75 & 10 & 2.0 \\
2 & Kangaroo 2 (no ears or tail) & 1.2 & 10 & 1.5 \\
3 & Kangaroo 3 (full outline) & 1.75 & 20 & 10.0 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Limits and normalizers for the three problems.}
\label{tab:problems}
\end{table}

Two things about this setup turned out to matter more than we expected. The
distance is not normalized for scale, so a curve of the right shape but the
wrong size scores badly. On the other hand, scaling every joint position by
the same factor scales the curve and the material together, which means each
mechanism comes with a free size knob that trades one objective for the other.
We use this everywhere. The other thing is that hypervolume is an area: what a
design contributes is its gain in distance multiplied by how much material
headroom sits above it. A small accuracy gain on a cheap mechanism is worth
more than the same gain on an expensive one, which we only really appreciated
after stage 4 below.

\subsection{Stage 1: guessing a lot}

We ran the starter notebook first. From a random start its GA found nothing
feasible for a 6-joint mechanism, and seeded with 100 random mechanisms it got
to a hypervolume of 0.2 on Kangaroo~2. Our reading of this was that the
optimizer was not the bottleneck. The search space of link layouts is huge and
most of it is useless, so before optimizing anything we wanted to see what
decent mechanisms even look like. We also had access to a cluster, and random
sampling is about the easiest thing there is to run in parallel, whereas a GA
population has to evolve together.

So the first stage just generates random mechanisms with 10 to 20 joints
(each new joint hangs off two earlier ones, as in the course randomizer, with
a few random joint layouts per link structure) and solves them in large
batches. Two tricks make this far more productive than it sounds. First, we do
not pick a traced joint in advance. Every moving joint of every mechanism is
treated as a candidate, and for each candidate we throw away all joints that
do not drive it, since those only add material. A random 15-joint mechanism
therefore gives us a handful of small candidates, and after pruning they had
between 4 and 9 joints. Second, the official metric is too slow to run on
everything, so candidates are first ranked by a cheap shape comparison done
with an FFT (least-squares alignment over all starting points, both
directions, rotation and scale). Only the best 40 per target in each batch go
to the official metric, and those are scored at nine different sizes so that
each one gives several points along its own distance and material trade-off.

Four workers ran this for about four hours each, which came to 20.9 million
mechanisms and 22 million official evaluations. With no optimization at all
the score was 2.78 (Figure~\ref{fig:screenfronts}). It was already 2.61 after
the first worker-hour, so most of those four hours bought very little, and in
hindsight we could have stopped much sooner.

\subsection{Stage 2: gradient descent on the good ones}

Next we took the most accurate screened mechanisms for each target and ran
Adam on their joint positions for 1500 steps. We picked a fixed number per
joint count instead of simply the top $N$, because otherwise the selection is
dominated by one size; this came to 136 mechanisms per target. Each was
optimized four times with a different weight on material,
\[
  \text{distance} + w \cdot \text{material}, \qquad w \in \{0,\ 0.03,\ 0.1,\ 0.3\},
\]
so that one starting point spreads out into several points on the front. We
also kept every intermediate iterate that was inside the limits, not just the
last one, which fills in the front for free.

This was the single biggest improvement after screening: 2.78 to 3.43, with
best distances down to 0.067, 0.30 and 0.48 (blue curves in
Figure~\ref{fig:progress}). Looking at the traced curves at this point,
Kangaroo~1 was essentially done. Kangaroo~3 had picked up both ears but its
tail and feet were smoothed over, and Kangaroo~2 was rounding off the snout
and the feet.

\subsection{Stage 3: growing mechanisms}

Gradient descent can move joints around but it cannot add a link, and a
7-joint mechanism is not going to draw a kangaroo's ears, tail and feet
however well its joints are placed. So we wrote a step that makes mechanisms
bigger. Each parent mechanism gets 96 children, and every child is the parent
plus one new joint, connected by two links, which becomes the new traced
point. The new joint is attached in one of three ways. It can go on the old
traced joint and one of that joint's parents, which puts it on the same rigid
body right next to the old tracer, so the child starts out drawing almost the
same curve and has one more degree of freedom to improve it. It can go on any
two existing joints. Or it can go on one moving joint and a new ground point.

The children are pruned, resized and scored, the best 512 get 250 Adam steps,
and the parents for the next generation are the most accurate mechanisms in
each joint-count bin (so that small ones are not crowded out by large ones).
After 20 generations the score was 3.50 and the best distances were 0.054,
0.22 and 0.46. Kangaroo~2 benefited most, going from 0.30 to 0.22. By the last
generation no child was beating its parent on any target.

We made a mistake in this run that is worth mentioning. Parents were selected
on distance only, and some of them drifted over the material limit, where
nothing they do counts. Kangaroo~1 reached a distance of 0.040 that way with a
mechanism we could not submit. We fixed the selection before running growth
again.

\subsection{Stage 4: a much wider refinement}

Two things bothered us about stage 2. It had started from only 136 mechanisms
per target, and the best results had come from a few of those that happened
to land in a good basin, so it seemed likely that we were leaving good starting
points unused. It also had no 4-joint mechanisms in it at all, because they
are never among the most accurate, and yet 4-joint mechanisms make up the
whole cheap end of every front.

So we reran refinement from about 4000 starting points per target: every seed
and front design from screening, everything stages 2 and 3 had produced, and
the best designs for the other two kangaroos (the three outlines are
variations on one shape, so we expected some transfer). To keep this
affordable we used successive halving, with a short run for all 4096, a longer
run for the best 1024, and a long run at several material weights for the best
192 plus 64 of the cheap ones.

The score went from 3.50 to 3.67, which was more than growth had given us. What
surprised us is that the best distance did not improve on any of the three
targets. The entire gain was from finding cheaper mechanisms at accuracies we
already had. That is the hypervolume-is-an-area point from Section~2 showing up
in practice.

\subsection{Stage 5: growth again}

Finally we ran the growth step once more, starting from the stage 4 results and
with parents kept inside the limits. This brought the score to 3.69. All three
design sets in the submitted file come out of this last run.

\section{Where the error is, and things that did not work}
\label{sec:error}

Once the best distances stopped moving we wanted to know where on the outline
the remaining error was, so we aligned our best designs to the target the same
way the scorer does and plotted the error at every target point
(Figure~\ref{fig:error3}).

Kangaroo~1 has nothing much left to fix. Kangaroo~2 has no single bad region;
its most accurate design has several peaks of similar height at the snout tip,
the feet and the lower-right corner, while cheaper 6-joint designs miss the
concave stretch between snout and feet. Kangaroo~3 is the clear case. Its
error is concentrated on the tail, at roughly 55 to 65\% of the way around the
perimeter, and for the most accurate design the worst fifth of the perimeter
accounts for 48\% of the distance.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{error_3.png}
\caption{Kangaroo 3, error at each target point (left) and along the perimeter
(right) for three designs. This was made partway through, on the evening of
October 5, so the numbers differ slightly from the final file. The peak near
60\% is the tail.}
\label{fig:error3}
\end{figure}

We think the reason the tail is so hard is in how the metric works. Points are
compared at matching positions along the two curves, so a traced curve that
pokes halfway into the tail does not get half the credit. It mostly gets its
other points pulled out of alignment. From the optimizer's side there is no
gentle slope that leads toward drawing a tail.

That led to three experiments, all on existing designs and all trying to lower
distance without changing the link layout. None of them worked
(Table~\ref{tab:negative}).

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{p{3.0cm}p{7.4cm}p{4.0cm}}
\toprule
Idea & What we did & Hypervolume change (K1 / K2 / K3) \\
\midrule
Flipping joints & Mirror a moving joint across the line through its two
parents (the other way of assembling the same links), then refine. About 7000
flips. & $+0.012$ / $+0.001$ / $+0.001$ \\
Softer loss & Optimize first on a two-way nearest-point distance, or on a
blurred target that is sharpened in steps, then switch to the real distance. &
Worse than or equal to a plain control on every target \\
Careful steps & Only accept a step if it improves one objective without
hurting the other. & $+0.0007$ / $+0.0005$ / $+0.0012$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Experiments that only move joints.}
\label{tab:negative}
\end{table}

The softer loss was the one we had hopes for, since it was meant to give
partial credit for a partly formed tail. We ran it against a control that used
the ordinary loss on the same parents, and the control won: for Kangaroo~3 it
reached a hypervolume of 24.36, against 24.27 and 24.19 for the two softer
variants. We also learned, a little painfully, that restarting Adam on a
design that has already converged tends to knock it out of its minimum and
leave it worse than it started, so one has to keep the best point seen and not
the final one.

Put together with stage 4, our conclusion is that the designs on our fronts
are at real local minima for the link layouts they have. The only thing that
was still lowering distance by the end was changing the layout, which is what
growth does.

\section{Discussion}

Looking back at Table~\ref{tab:stages}, random screening alone got us 2.78 out
of 3.69 and the first refinement added another 0.65. Everything we did after
that, which was most of the effort, added 0.26. If we were short on time we
would do stages 1 and 2 and stop.

The fronts for the three targets look quite different. For Kangaroo~1 the most
accurate and best balanced designs are close in distance (0.052 and 0.092) but
the accurate one uses more than four times the material, so the last bit of
accuracy is very expensive. Kangaroo~2 is similar but milder. Kangaroo~3 does
not behave like this. Its most accurate and best balanced designs are close in
both distance and material, and the front simply stops at about 0.44. It is
not the material limit that stops it, since the most accurate design uses 4.91
out of 20. That design does have 19 joints, and we wondered whether the
20-joint limit was what held us back, but the ten joints it has beyond the
best balanced design only take the distance from 0.473 to 0.441. We do not
think more joints would have fixed the tail. What is missing is the right kind
of link layout, and neither random screening nor our growth step found it.

One oddity in the submission is that the lightest Kangaroo~3 design is a bare
crank with two joints, whose circle happens to fall just inside the distance
limit. The course scorer accepts it. The submission page says mechanisms
should have 4 to 20 joints, so it may not be counted there; it accounts for
0.005 of that problem's hypervolume, so it matters very little either way.

If we had more time we would go after the tail directly. One option is to
score growth candidates by their error on the tail stretch of the perimeter
instead of the total distance, so that a child that starts to form a tail is
kept even if it is worse overall. Another is to search separately for small
sub-mechanisms that draw a narrow spike and attach those to the best body we
have.

\section{Compute and code}

Everything ran as CPU jobs on MIT's Engaging cluster, mostly on the
preemptable partition. Jobs there can be killed at any moment, so all our
scripts save a checkpoint about once a minute and pick up from it when they
restart. Each stage merges its designs with the best submission so far,
rescoring with the course scorer and keeping the result only if it is better.
The code is at \url{\repourl}; \texttt{CODE.md} there lists what each
script does and the commands to rerun the pipeline.
